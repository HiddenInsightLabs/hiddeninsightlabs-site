The Testing Problem That Comes With AI Personalisation at Scale

Apps like Duolingo have made a serious, sophisticated bet on AI-driven personalised experiences. That ambition is genuinely impressive — and it creates a testing challenge that traditional QA simply wasn't built to handle.

Hidden InSight Labs · 9 min read

Duolingo is one of the most carefully engineered consumer apps in the world. Its approach to AI-driven learning — adapting lesson sequences, difficulty curves, and exercise types to individual user behaviour in real time — represents a serious, sustained investment in personalisation at a scale very few companies have attempted. The product has over 500 million registered users and a genuine commitment to using machine learning to improve learning outcomes, not just engagement metrics.

It's worth taking seriously, because what Duolingo has built is where a significant portion of the software industry is heading. Personalisation driven by AI — not just surface customisation, but genuine adaptation of the core experience based on individual behaviour — is becoming a standard expectation rather than a premium feature. Recommendation engines, adaptive learning systems, personalised content feeds, dynamically generated UI flows: these are no longer the exclusive domain of the largest tech companies.

And they all share a testing problem that most teams haven't fully confronted yet — the kind of blind spot an independent second opinion exists to find before your users do.

What AI Personalisation Actually Changes About the Product

Traditional software, for all its complexity, has a property that makes it testable: given the same inputs, it produces the same outputs. A QA team can define test cases, run them systematically, and verify the software behaves as specified. The state space is large but bounded.

AI personalisation breaks this property at a fundamental level. When the experience a user receives is shaped by their individual history and inferred preferences, no two users see quite the same product. The lesson sequence generated for a 40-year-old professional learning Spanish for a trip is different from the one generated for a teenager learning the same language with a gaming motivation. Both are generated by the same system. Both need to work. But they cannot both be tested by running the same test case.

The combinatorial problem: in a traditionally structured app, the number of distinct experiences a user can have is large but finite. In an AI-personalised app, the number of distinct experiences is effectively unbounded. You cannot enumerate the test cases. You have to think differently about what testing means.

How the Industry Got Here

The shift toward AI-driven personalisation has happened gradually enough that its testing implications have crept up on the industry rather than arriving as a clear discontinuity. Teams building personalisation features incrementally have generally extended their existing testing practices to cover each new feature individually. The cumulative effect on the testability of the overall experience has been less visible.

Duolingo's well-documented pivot toward an "AI-first" approach — leaning further into AI-generated content and personalised learning paths — is a useful marker because it made the stakes explicit. The more genuinely adaptive a product becomes, the less useful conventional test coverage becomes as a quality assurance strategy. That isn't a flaw in the approach. It's a consequence of the ambition.

Five Specific Challenges AI Personalisation Creates for Testing

01

You can't test the experience — only the system

Automated testing can verify the model runs, API calls succeed, and content renders. It cannot verify whether the specific experience a specific user receives is actually good — coherent, well-paced, accurately calibrated. That judgment requires a human, encountering the generated experience as a real user would.

02

Edge users are invisible until they appear

AI models perform well near the centre of their training distribution and less predictably at the edges — users whose behaviour wasn't well represented in training data. These edge cases don't appear in synthetic testing. They appear when real, diverse users encounter the system.

03

Quality is longitudinal, not transactional

For a learning app, quality isn't visible in a single session. Does the system correctly identify a struggling user and adjust? Does it avoid repeating content already mastered? These qualities require extended engagement from evaluators to surface.

04

Experience varies by device and context the model doesn't see

Personalisation models typically optimise on behavioural signals, not the device or network the user is on. An experience that feels smooth on a high-end device on fast WiFi may feel fragmented on a mid-range device on a constrained connection.

05

Model updates create invisible regressions

When the underlying model is retrained or fine-tuned, the change is distributed across millions of individually generated journeys. There's no single feature to regression-test — only real user journeys, evaluated by real people, before and after the change.

What Duolingo Gets Right — and What the Industry Can Learn

Duolingo has invested heavily in what it calls its learning engine — not just the model, but the feedback infrastructure around it. It runs large numbers of simultaneous A/B tests, uses engagement and retention as proxies for learning quality while acknowledging their limits, and publishes research on the pedagogical validity of its approach. Few teams building AI-personalised software have matched this level of investment.

The lesson is that AI personalisation demands a different quality infrastructure, not just better automated testing. Metrics and experiments operate at a population level: they tell you version B retains users better than version A, on average. They don't tell you that version B introduced a failure mode for a specific class of users that version A didn't have.

Population-level signals

What A/B tests and metrics reveal: whether one variant performs better than another, on average, across the full user base.

Useful for optimising aggregate outcomes and identifying broad regressions.

Limitation: averages can improve while specific populations get worse.

Individual-level signals

What human evaluation reveals: whether specific users, with specific profiles, have a good experience — or a broken one.

Useful for surfacing edge-user failure modes and longitudinal experience quality.

Limitation: doesn't scale to the full user base — works best as structured sampling.

These two approaches aren't in competition. They're complementary. Population-level signals tell you where to look. Individual-level human evaluation tells you what specific users actually encounter.

A Framework for Testing AI-Personalised Products

Teams that handle this well think about evaluation across three distinct dimensions:

  1. System correctness — does the personalisation infrastructure work as specified? Amenable to automated testing.
  2. Population-level quality — does the personalised experience produce better aggregate outcomes? The domain of A/B testing and retention analysis.
  3. Individual-level experience quality — do specific users, in specific contexts, actually have a good experience? This requires human evaluation.

Most teams invest heavily in the first dimension, reasonably in the second, and inconsistently in the third — which is exactly where the most consequential quality failures hide.

The Diversity Imperative

An AI personalisation model's behaviour for a given type of user is a function, in part, of how well that type of user was represented in training data. Users at the margins — different demographics, learning styles, device and connectivity profiles — get experiences that were less directly optimised for.

This means an evaluation panel drawn from the same pool as the development team will systematically miss the failure modes that affect users who are different from that pool. The value of a genuinely diverse panel is not just ethical. It's epistemic — a diverse panel surfaces failure modes a homogeneous one cannot.

For a product like Duolingo — whose fastest-growing user base spans markets like Brazil, India, and across Sub-Saharan Africa — this is particularly consequential. A panel that includes learners from those markets, on the hardware and network conditions normal there, reveals whether the AI-personalised experience is actually working for them.

Where This Leaves Teams Building AI-Personalised Products

The practical implication isn't that AI personalisation is too risky to pursue. It's that pursuing it responsibly requires an expanded conception of quality assurance — one that takes the limitations of automated testing and population-level metrics seriously, and fills the gaps with structured human evaluation.

That evaluation doesn't have to be massive or continuous. A panel of real users representing the diversity of your actual user base, assessed by evaluators who can judge quality in context, done periodically and particularly before major model updates, closes a gap that no amount of automated testing can address.
